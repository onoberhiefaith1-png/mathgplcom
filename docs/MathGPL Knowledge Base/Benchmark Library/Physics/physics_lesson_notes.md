## Lesson 1: Physical Quantities, SI Units and Measurement

**Subject:** Physics · **Class:** SS1 / Year 10 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Students can read a ruler and do simple multiplication.
**Instructional materials:** Metre rule, vernier caliper, micrometer screw gauge, stopwatch, beam balance.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Distinguish between fundamental and derived quantities.
2. State the SI unit of each fundamental quantity.
3. Convert values using SI prefixes.
4. Read a vernier caliper correctly.

### Introduction
Ask the class: "If I say the table is 2 long, what is missing?" Students notice that a number without a unit has no meaning. Physics depends on measuring things accurately and agreeing on units.

### Explanation
A **physical quantity** is anything that can be measured and expressed as a number with a unit.

**Fundamental quantities** do not depend on other quantities:

| Quantity | Symbol | SI unit | Unit symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | l | metre | m |
| Mass | m | kilogram | kg |
| Time | t | second | s |
| Temperature | T | kelvin | K |
| Electric current | I | ampere | A |
| Amount of substance | n | mole | mol |
| Luminous intensity | Iᵥ | candela | cd |

**Derived quantities** are formed from fundamental ones, for example speed (m/s), area (m²), density (kg/m³) and force (N = kg·m/s²).

**SI prefixes:** kilo (k) = 10³, centi (c) = 10⁻², milli (m) = 10⁻³, micro (µ) = 10⁻⁶.

**Vernier caliper reading** = main scale reading + vernier coincidence × 0.01 cm.

### Worked Example 1
Convert 2.5 km to metres.

**Solution**
2.5 km = 2.5 × 10³ m
2.5 km = 2500 m

### Worked Example 2
A vernier caliper shows a main scale reading of 3.2 cm, and the 6th vernier division coincides. Find the reading.

**Solution**
Reading = main scale + vernier coincidence × 0.01 cm
Reading = 3.2 cm + 6 × 0.01 cm
Reading = 3.2 cm + 0.06 cm
Reading = 3.26 cm

### Exercise
1. List four fundamental quantities and their SI units.
2. Convert 450 mg to kilograms.
3. Express 0.000 25 m in micrometres.
4. A vernier caliper shows a main scale reading of 5.4 cm, and the 3rd vernier division coincides. Find the reading.

### Conclusion
Every measurement needs a number and a unit. There are seven fundamental SI quantities, and all other quantities are derived from them. Prefixes let us write very large and very small values neatly.

## Lesson 2: Motion: Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

**Class:** SS1 / Year 10 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Distance, time and units.
**Materials:** Trolley, ramp, stopwatch, metre rule, graph paper.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define speed, velocity and acceleration.
2. Apply v = u + at and s = ut + ½at².
3. Interpret a velocity–time graph.

### Introduction
A bus travels from Lagos to Ibadan. Ask: "How do we describe how fast it moved, and in which direction?"

### Explanation
- **Speed** = distance ÷ time (m/s). Speed is a scalar.
- **Velocity** = displacement ÷ time (m/s). Velocity is a vector, so it has a direction.
- **Acceleration** a = (v − u) ÷ t (m/s²), where u = initial velocity and v = final velocity.

**Equations of uniformly accelerated motion:**
v = u + at
s = ut + ½at²
v² = u² + 2as

On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is the acceleration and the area under the graph is the displacement.

### Worked Example 1
A car starts from rest and accelerates at 3 m/s² for 5 s. Find its final velocity.

**Solution**
Given: u = 0 m/s, a = 3 m/s², t = 5 s
Required: v
v = u + at
v = 0 m/s + (3 m/s²)(5 s)
v = 15 m/s

### Worked Example 2
Find the distance covered by the same car in that time.

**Solution**
s = ut + ½at²
s = (0 m/s)(5 s) + ½(3 m/s²)(5 s)²
s = 0 m + ½(3 m/s²)(25 s²)
s = 37.5 m

### Exercise
1. Distinguish between speed and velocity.
2. A runner covers 400 m in 50 s. Find her average speed.
3. A body moving at 10 m/s accelerates at 2 m/s² for 4 s. Find its final velocity.
4. A train decelerates from 20 m/s to rest in 10 s. Find its deceleration.

### Conclusion
Speed and velocity describe how fast something moves; velocity also gives the direction. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. The equations of motion link u, v, a, s and t.

## Lesson 3: Forces and Newton's Laws of Motion

**Class:** SS1 / Year 10 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Mass, acceleration and vectors.
**Materials:** Spring balance, trolley, masses, string.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. State Newton's three laws.
2. Draw a free-body diagram.
3. Calculate the resultant force and acceleration using F = ma.

### Introduction
Push a book across the desk; it stops. Ask: "Why does it stop, and what would happen on ice?"

### Explanation
A **force** F is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N).
- **First law:** a body stays at rest or in uniform motion unless a resultant force acts on it (inertia).
- **Second law:** resultant force = mass × acceleration, F = ma.
- **Third law:** to every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.

**Weight:** W = mg, where g = 10 m/s². In a free-body diagram, draw arrows from the body showing the weight (down), the normal reaction R (up), the applied force and friction.

### Worked Example
A 4 kg box is pulled with a force of 30 N against a friction of 10 N. Find its acceleration.

**Solution**
Given: m = 4 kg, F = 30 N, friction = 10 N
Resultant force = 30 N − 10 N
Resultant force = 20 N
a = F ÷ m
a = 20 N ÷ 4 kg
a = 5 m/s²

### Exercise
1. State Newton's second law of motion.
2. Find the weight of a 60 kg student (g = 10 m/s²).
3. A force of 50 N gives a body an acceleration of 2.5 m/s². Find its mass.
4. Draw a labelled free-body diagram of a book resting on a table.

### Conclusion
Forces change the motion of bodies. Newton's laws explain inertia, F = ma, and action–reaction pairs. Free-body diagrams show every force acting on a body.

## Lesson 4: Work, Energy and Power

**Class:** SS2 / Year 10 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Force and distance.
**Materials:** Masses, metre rule, stopwatch, pulley.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define work, energy and power.
2. Calculate kinetic and potential energy.
3. State the principle of conservation of energy.

### Introduction
Lift a bag onto a shelf. Ask: "Did you do work? What happened to the energy you used?"

### Explanation
- **Work** W = Fd, measured in joules (J). Work is done when a force moves its point of application.
- **Kinetic energy:** Eₖ = ½mv².
- **Gravitational potential energy:** Eₚ = mgh.
- **Power** P = W ÷ t, measured in watts (W).
- **Conservation of energy:** energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.
- **Efficiency** η = (useful output ÷ total input) × 100%.

### Worked Example
A 2 kg ball is dropped from 5 m. Find its potential energy and its speed just before it hits the ground (g = 10 m/s²).

**Solution**
Eₚ = mgh
Eₚ = (2 kg)(10 m/s²)(5 m)
Eₚ = 100 J

Just before impact, Eₖ = Eₚ:
½mv² = 100 J
½(2 kg)v² = 100 J
v² = 100 m²/s²
v = 10 m/s

### Exercise
1. Define power and state its SI unit.
2. A force of 40 N moves a box 3 m. Find the work done.
3. A pump does 6000 J of work in 30 s. Find its power.
4. A machine has an input of 500 J and an output of 400 J. Find its efficiency.

### Conclusion
Work transfers energy. Energy is conserved, changing between kinetic, potential and other forms. Power is the rate of doing work.

## Lesson 5: Density and Pressure

**Class:** SS1 / Year 10 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Mass, volume and area.
**Materials:** Measuring cylinder, beam balance, stone, water.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define density and pressure.
2. Calculate density and pressure in solids and liquids.
3. Explain upthrust.

### Introduction
Ask: "Why does a sharp knife cut better than a blunt one? Why does a ship float but a nail sinks?"

### Explanation
- **Density** ρ = m ÷ V, measured in kg/m³.
- **Pressure** P = F ÷ A, measured in pascals (Pa = N/m²).
- **Liquid pressure:** P = ρgh, where h is the depth.
- **Archimedes' principle:** a body immersed in a fluid experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of fluid displaced.

### Worked Example 1
A stone of mass 0.6 kg has a volume of 0.0002 m³. Find its density.

**Solution**
ρ = m ÷ V
ρ = 0.6 kg ÷ 0.0002 m³
ρ = 3000 kg/m³

### Worked Example 2
Find the pressure at a depth of 5 m in water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 m/s²).

**Solution**
P = ρgh
P = (1000 kg/m³)(10 m/s²)(5 m)
P = 50 000 Pa

### Exercise
1. State Archimedes' principle.
2. A block of mass 2 kg has a volume of 0.001 m³. Find its density.
3. A force of 200 N acts on an area of 0.5 m². Find the pressure.
4. Explain why dams are built thicker at the bottom.

### Conclusion
Density compares mass with volume. Pressure is force per unit area, and in liquids it increases with depth. Upthrust explains why objects float.

## Lesson 6: Heat and Temperature

**Class:** SS2 / Year 11 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Energy and temperature scales.
**Materials:** Thermometer, beaker, heater, water, stopwatch.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Distinguish between heat and temperature.
2. Use Q = mcΔT.
3. Explain specific latent heat.

### Introduction
Ask: "Why does a large pot of water take longer to boil than a cup, on the same fire?"

### Explanation
- **Temperature** measures how hot a body is (K or °C).
- **Heat** Q is the energy transferred because of a temperature difference (J).
- **Specific heat capacity:** Q = mcΔT, where c is in J/(kg·K).
- **Latent heat:** Q = mL, where L is in J/kg. This energy changes the state of a substance without changing its temperature.
- **Converting temperature:** T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.

### Worked Example
How much heat raises 2 kg of water from 20 °C to 70 °C? (c = 4200 J/(kg·K))

**Solution**
ΔT = 70 °C − 20 °C
ΔT = 50 K
Q = mcΔT
Q = (2 kg)(4200 J/(kg·K))(50 K)
Q = 420 000 J

### Exercise
1. Distinguish between heat and temperature.
2. Convert 27 °C to kelvin.
3. Find the heat needed to raise 0.5 kg of copper by 40 K (c = 400 J/(kg·K)).
4. Find the heat needed to melt 0.2 kg of ice (L = 336 000 J/kg).

### Conclusion
Temperature measures hotness, while heat is energy in transfer. Q = mcΔT is used for temperature changes, and Q = mL is used for changes of state.

## Lesson 7: Waves

**Class:** SS2 / Year 11 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Frequency and period.
**Materials:** Slinky spring, ripple tank, rope.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Distinguish between transverse and longitudinal waves.
2. Label the wavelength and amplitude on a wave diagram.
3. Use v = fλ.

### Introduction
Shake one end of a rope. Ask: "What travels along the rope: the rope itself or energy?"

### Explanation
A **wave** transfers energy without transferring matter.
- **Transverse waves:** the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction of travel (light, water waves).
- **Longitudinal waves:** the particles vibrate along the direction of travel (sound).
- **Wavelength** λ (m), **amplitude** A (m), **frequency** f (Hz) and **period** T (s), with T = 1 ÷ f.
- **Wave equation:** v = fλ.

### Worked Example
A sound wave has a frequency of 340 Hz and a wavelength of 1 m. Find its speed and period.

**Solution**
v = fλ
v = (340 Hz)(1 m)
v = 340 m/s
T = 1 ÷ f
T = 1 ÷ 340 Hz
T ≈ 0.0029 s

### Exercise
1. Give two examples each of transverse and longitudinal waves.
2. A wave has a speed of 20 m/s and a wavelength of 4 m. Find its frequency.
3. Draw a transverse wave and label its wavelength and amplitude.
4. Why can sound not travel through a vacuum?

### Conclusion
Waves carry energy. They are either transverse or longitudinal, and their speed, frequency and wavelength are linked by v = fλ.

## Lesson 8: Refraction of Light

**Class:** SS2 / Year 11 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Reflection and angles.
**Materials:** Glass block, ray box, protractor, pins.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Define refraction and state Snell's law.
2. Draw a ray diagram through a glass block.
3. Calculate the refractive index.

### Introduction
A pencil in a glass of water looks bent. Ask: "Why?"

### Explanation
**Refraction** is the bending of light as it passes from one medium into another, because its speed changes.
- In the ray diagram, i = angle of incidence and r = angle of refraction, both measured from the normal.
- **Snell's law:** n = sin i ÷ sin r, where n is the refractive index (no unit).
- Light bends towards the normal when it enters a denser medium.

### Worked Example
Light enters glass at i = 45° and is refracted at r = 28°. Find the refractive index.

**Solution**
n = sin i ÷ sin r
n = sin 45° ÷ sin 28°
n = 0.707 ÷ 0.469
n ≈ 1.51

### Exercise
1. State Snell's law.
2. Light enters water at i = 30°, and n = 1.33. Find r.
3. Draw a labelled ray diagram for light passing through a rectangular glass block.
4. Explain why a pool appears shallower than it really is.

### Conclusion
Light bends when its speed changes between media. Snell's law gives the refractive index, n = sin i ÷ sin r.

## Lesson 9: Electric Circuits and Ohm's Law

**Class:** SS2 / Year 11 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Charge and current.
**Materials:** Cells, resistors, ammeter, voltmeter, switch, connecting wires.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. State Ohm's law.
2. Draw series and parallel circuits correctly.
3. Calculate the effective resistance, current and power.

### Introduction
Ask: "When one bulb in a string of festive lights fails, why do they all go off?"

### Explanation
- **Current** I (A), **potential difference** V (V) and **resistance** R (Ω).
- **Ohm's law:** V = IR, provided the temperature stays constant.
- **Series:** R = R₁ + R₂, and the current is the same everywhere.
- **Parallel:** 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂, and the p.d. is the same across each branch.
- **Power:** P = VI = I²R.
- In a circuit diagram, every component must sit on a closed loop. The ammeter goes in series and the voltmeter in parallel.

### Worked Example
Resistors of 4 Ω and 6 Ω are in series with a 12 V battery. Find the total resistance, the current and the power.

**Solution**
R = R₁ + R₂
R = 4 Ω + 6 Ω
R = 10 Ω
I = V ÷ R
I = 12 V ÷ 10 Ω
I = 1.2 A
P = VI
P = (12 V)(1.2 A)
P = 14.4 W

### Exercise
1. State Ohm's law.
2. Find the effective resistance of 3 Ω and 6 Ω in parallel.
3. A 240 V lamp draws 0.5 A. Find its resistance and power.
4. Draw a circuit with a cell, a switch, two lamps in parallel and an ammeter.

### Conclusion
V = IR links voltage, current and resistance. Series and parallel connections change the total resistance, and power measures how quickly electrical energy is used.

## Lesson 10: Radioactivity

**Class:** SS3 / Year 11 · **Duration:** 45 minutes
**Previous knowledge:** Atomic structure.
**Materials:** Chart of radiation types, simulation, periodic table.

### Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:
1. Describe alpha, beta and gamma radiation.
2. Write nuclear decay equations.
3. Solve half-life problems.

### Introduction
Ask: "How can scientists tell the age of an ancient bone?"

### Explanation
A nuclide is written as ᴬ_Z X, where A = nucleon number and Z = proton number.
- **Alpha (α):** a helium nucleus, ⁴₂He. A decreases by 4 and Z decreases by 2. Stopped by paper.
- **Beta (β):** a fast electron, ⁰₋₁e. Z increases by 1. Stopped by aluminium.
- **Gamma (γ):** an electromagnetic wave. A and Z are unchanged. Reduced by lead.
- **Half-life** t½: the time for half of the nuclei to decay.

### Worked Example 1
Complete the equation: ²³⁸₉₂U → ? + ⁴₂He

**Solution**
A = 238 − 4
A = 234
Z = 92 − 2
Z = 90
²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He

### Worked Example 2
A sample of 80 g has a half-life of 5 days. Find the mass left after 15 days.

**Solution**
Number of half-lives = 15 days ÷ 5 days
Number of half-lives = 3
Mass left = 80 g × (½)³
Mass left = 80 g × ⅛
Mass left = 10 g

### Exercise
1. Compare alpha, beta and gamma radiation by their penetrating power.
2. Complete: ²¹⁴₈₂Pb → ? + ⁰₋₁e
3. A sample of 64 g has a half-life of 2 hours. Find the mass left after 8 hours.
4. State two uses of radioactivity.

### Conclusion
Unstable nuclei emit alpha, beta or gamma radiation. Decay equations conserve the nucleon number and the proton number, and half-life measures how quickly a sample decays.
