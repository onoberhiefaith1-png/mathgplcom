Question 10. What does the word “squared” mean? Write the square of \(3\) in index notation.

Solution

Squared means multiplying a number by itself.

\[ 3\times3=\boxed{3^2} \]

Question 11. What does the word “cubed” mean? Write the cube of \(4\) in index notation.

Solution

Cubed means multiplying a number by itself three times as a factor.

\[ 4\times4\times4=\boxed{4^3} \]

Question 12. A learner writes \(3+3+3+3\) as \(3^4\). Explain the difference between the sum and the power.

Solution

Repeated addition:

\[ 3+3+3+3=12 \]

Repeated multiplication:

\[ 3^4=3\times3\times3\times3=81 \]

Since

\[ 12\ne81 \]

the learner is incorrect. Addition and exponentiation are different operations.

Question 1. Find every ordered pair of integers \((a,n)\), with \(a\geq2\) and \(n\geq2\), for which \(a^n=81\).

Solution

\[ a^n=81 \]

Express \(81\) as repeated multiplication:

\[ 81=3\times3\times3\times3 \]

\[ 81=3^4 \]

Alternatively,

\[ 81=9\times9=9^2 \]

Since \(81=3^4\), the exponent \(n\) must be a divisor of \(4\). With \(n\geq2\), the possibilities are \(2\) and \(4\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(a,n)=(3,4),(9,2)} \]

Question 2. Find every ordered pair of integers \((a,n)\), with \(a\geq2\) and \(n\geq2\), for which \(a^n=256\).

Solution

\[ a^n=256 \]

Express \(256\) as a power of \(2\):

\[ 256=2^8 \]

For the base to be an integer, \(n\) must divide \(8\).

The possible values are:

\[ n=2,\ 4,\ 8 \]

When \(n=2\):

\[ a^2=256=16^2 \]

\[ a=16 \]

When \(n=4\):

\[ a^4=256=4^4 \]

\[ a=4 \]

When \(n=8\):

\[ a^8=256=2^8 \]

\[ a=2 \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{(a,n)=(16,2),(4,4),(2,8)} \]

Question 8. Find every positive integer \(n\) for which \(n^2=2^n\). Explain how to rule out larger \(n\), rather than only checking a few small values.

Solution

\[ n^2=2^n \]

Test the first positive integers:

For \(n=1\):

\[ 1^2=1,\qquad2^1=2 \]

Not equal.

For \(n=2\):

\[ 2^2=4,\qquad2^2=4 \]

Valid.

For \(n=3\):

\[ 3^2=9,\qquad2^3=8 \]

Not equal.

For \(n=4\):

\[ 4^2=16,\qquad2^4=16 \]

Valid.

For \(n=5\):

\[ 5^2=25<32=2^5 \]

Now show that \(2^n\) stays greater than \(n^2\) for every \(n\geq5\).

Suppose

\[ 2^n>n^2 \]

Multiplying both sides by \(2\):

\[ 2^{n+1}>2n^2 \]

For \(n\geq5\),

\[ 2n^2>(n+1)^2 \]

because

\[ 2n^2-(n+1)^2=n^2-2n-1>0 \]

Therefore,

\[ 2^{n+1}>(n+1)^2 \]

So no integer greater than \(4\) can satisfy the original equation.

\[ \boxed{n=2,4} \]

Question 9. A number is both a square and a cube of positive integers and is greater than \(1\) but less than \(100\). Find it and give both representations.

Solution

A number that is both a square and a cube must be a sixth power.

\[ 2^6=64 \]

The next sixth power is

\[ 3^6=729 \]

Since \(729>100\), the required number is \(64\).

As a square:

\[ 64=8^2 \]

As a cube:

\[ 64=4^3 \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{64=8^2=4^3} \]

Question 12. Find positive integers \(a\) and \(n\), with \(a\geq2\) and \(n\geq2\), such that \(a^n=216\). List every valid pair.

Solution

\[ a^n=216 \]

Express \(216\) as prime factors:

\[ 216=2\times2\times2\times3\times3\times3 \]

Group the factors:

\[ 216=(2\times3)(2\times3)(2\times3) \]

\[ 216=6\times6\times6 \]

\[ 216=6^3 \]

Therefore,

\[ a=6,\qquad n=3 \]

To check whether other pairs are possible, consider \(n=2\):

\[ 14^2=196<216<225=15^2 \]

So \(216\) is not a perfect square.

Also,

\[ 216=2^3\times3^3 \]

For \(216\) to be an integer power with exponent \(n\), both prime-factor exponents must be divisible by \(n\). Since both are \(3\), and \(n\geq2\), the only possible exponent is \(3\).

Hence the only valid pair is

\[ \boxed{(a,n)=(6,3)} \]
