### How each stage is described
**Build** = what gets made. **Significance** = what teachers and students will be able to do and how it feels to use; what they can solve; what they learn; the real-world problem it answers; and why it beats the traditional method.

---

### Stage 1 — Rename to Math Lab and check the foundation (do this first)
**Build:** Rename everywhere (sidebar, page, title, address `/math-lab`, with `/graph` forwarding to it). Check that the existing graph in Lesson Notes and on the Smartboard is unchanged. Set up one shared equation reader that every later stage uses. It handles fractions, powers, roots, trig, logs, |x|, π, implicit multiplication like 2x, and clear error messages. Start a written progress record.
**Significance:** Teachers get one clearly named place for exploring maths, without risk to their lesson notes. Every later tool understands equations the same way, so a teacher who types `y = 2x² − 3` gets the same answer in every mode. This is the reliability layer: without it, a wrong graph would teach a wrong idea.

### Stage 2 — Smart equation entry and automatic plotting
**Build:** Type an equation and Math Lab works out what it is: a 2D curve, a 3D surface, an implicit shape, a parametric curve or a point. It picks 2D, 3D or ND itself and chooses a sensible window, so a parabola's turning point and roots are visible. Equations can be edited, shown together, hidden or removed. Includes zoom limits, smart tick spacing, equal-scale and Reset View.
**Significance:**
- **Experience:** type, press Plot, and the right picture appears, framed properly.
- **Can solve:** finding roots, intersections and turning points by sight, then checking them.
- **Learns:** that every equation has a shape, and how changing a number changes that shape.
- **Real world:** reading charts of growth, cost and motion.
- **Better than paper:** no table of values and no hand-chosen scale, so a whole lesson's worth of sketches takes minutes and errors from plotting by hand disappear.

### Stage 3 — The full Equation Library with guided lessons
**Build:** A large ready-made library covering the topics in your master document's Session 4: linear, quadratic, cubic, polynomial, reciprocal, exponential, logarithmic, trig and inverse trig, modulus, piecewise, inequalities, circles and conics, parametric, polar, 3D surfaces, planes and spheres, matrices, nD, calculus and ODE examples. Each entry has its category, a default view, **parameter sliders** (e.g. a, b, c), and a short "What to investigate / why it matters" card, as your Session 18 asks. Search and tabs as in your picture.
**Significance:** A teacher prepares a demonstration in seconds instead of drawing it. Students move a slider and watch "a" stretch a parabola or "k" shift a sine wave, so they discover a rule rather than memorise it. The "why it matters" card links each curve to its use, for example exponentials and compound interest or sine and sound waves. A textbook shows one frozen example; Math Lab shows every version.

### Stage 4 — Mobile precision pointer, points, lines and vectors
**Build:** On phones and tablets, a circular handle with a fine tip that is dragged and confirmed, so a finger never hides the point. It can snap to grid points, roots and intersections, and can be cancelled. Coordinates stay exact at any zoom. Also: plotting and labelling points, the line through two points, midpoint, gradient, distance, and vectors with addition and scaling.
**Significance:** Students on phones can place (2, −3) exactly, which is impossible with a fat finger on a normal graph. They solve coordinate geometry such as gradients, midpoints, lengths and the equation of a line, and see vectors add tip-to-tail. Real-world links are navigation, maps and forces. It beats paper because every measurement is exact and checked straight away.

### Stage 5 — Full 3D graph: planes, intersections and cross-sections
**Build:** A polished lightweight 3D view as in your picture: points with drop lines, 3D lines, planes, surfaces, spheres, two planes meeting in a line, three planes meeting at a point (3×3 simultaneous equations), and cross-section slices of a surface at a chosen height. Touch: one finger rotates, two fingers pan and zoom. Lower detail on phones.
**Significance:** Students finally see why 2x + y − z = 4 is a flat plane, and why a system of three equations has one, none or infinitely many solutions. They can turn the scene and look from any side. Real-world links are architecture, engineering drawings, terrain maps and 3D printing. A drawing on paper cannot be rotated, so depth is guessed; here it is shown.

### Stage 6 — Matrix and linear algebra lab
**Build:** Enter a 2×2 or 3×3 matrix and watch it transform a grid, a unit square and a picture: scaling, rotation, reflection, shear and projection. Shows the determinant as the change in area or volume, eigenvectors as directions that do not turn, the inverse, combining matrices, and solving systems with matrices. Every number is calculated exactly, with steps.
**Significance:** This answers your document's key point: students can calculate eigenvalues yet not know what they mean. Here they *see* that det = 0 squashes space flat, and that eigenvectors are the lines that stay put. Real-world links are computer graphics, robotics, Google's ranking, image compression and vibrations in bridges. Hand calculation gives a number; the lab gives its meaning.

### Stage 7 — Higher dimensions: 4D, 5D, 6D and nD
**Build:** Building on the current cube view: tesseract and n-cubes, n-simplex and other shapes. Projection (perspective or flat), 3D slices through 4D objects, rotation in any plane of axes with sliders, and colour as an extra dimension. The true nD coordinates stay exact while only the picture is projected.
**Significance:** Students meet something impossible to draw by hand and learn to reason by analogy: a square's shadow is to a cube as a cube's shadow is to a tesseract. It builds abstract thinking for university maths, physics and data science, where data with many variables is normal. No paper method can show this at all.

### Stage 8 — Calculus lab and Riemann sums
**Build:**
- Limits approached step by step.
- A tangent line sliding along a curve, with its gradient drawn live as the derivative graph.
- Stationary points, plus concavity from the second derivative.
- The area under a curve shown as rectangles (left, right, midpoint, trapezium) with a slider for n, converging to the exact integral.
- Area between curves, and volumes of revolution in 3D.

**Significance:** Students watch the derivative being made and watch the rectangles fill the area as n grows. They understand *why* integration measures accumulation, not only how to do it. They can solve optimisation, rates of change, areas and volumes. Real-world links are speed from distance, total distance from speed, cost and profit optimisation, and medicine dosage. The chalkboard version draws just one tangent; here there are infinitely many.

### Stage 9 — Real analysis
**Build:** Sequences and series plotted term by term, convergence with an ε-band (pick ε, see N), the ε–δ definition of continuity and limits made interactive, partial sums of series, Taylor polynomials hugging a function, and counter-examples such as a jump or a function that oscillates without settling.
**Significance:** University-style rigour becomes visible: students set ε and find the δ themselves. They learn when a picture is enough and when proof takes over, which your document asks for explicitly. Real-world links are how calculators compute sin x (Taylor series) and how much error an approximation carries. Proofs written on paper are abstract; here each symbol has a picture.

### Stage 10 — Differential equations
**Build:**
- Slope fields for dy/dx = f(x, y).
- Tap a starting point to draw its solution curve.
- Euler and Runge–Kutta step by step, with the error shown.
- Equilibria and their stability.
- Classic models: growth and decay, logistic, cooling, predator–prey, spring and pendulum.
- Phase plane for systems.

**Significance:** Students see every possible solution at once and how the starting value decides which one happens. They learn the model, not only the method. Real-world links are population growth, epidemics, cooling coffee, interest, circuits and orbits. Solving by hand gives a formula only for special cases; the lab handles any equation and shows its behaviour.

### Stage 11 — Complex analysis, geometry, probability, statistics and numerical methods
**Build:**
- Argand diagram, complex arithmetic as rotation and scaling, and roots of unity.
- Domain colouring and contour maps of complex functions.
- Geometric transformations, loci and conics from their definitions.
- Probability distributions with sliders, and a live normal curve.
- Regression lines through data.
- Numerical methods: Newton–Raphson and bisection, step by step.

**Significance:** Multiplying by i becomes a visible quarter turn. Probability curves move as the mean and standard deviation change. Students watch Newton's method zoom in on a root. Real-world links are electrical engineering, signal processing, quality control, polls and machine learning. These topics are usually left as formulas; here each one is an experiment.

### Stage 12 — Saving, students, offline, integration and final review (do this last)
**Build:**
- Teachers save and name Math Lab scenes and share them with a class.
- Students open them and can only use the controls the teacher allows.
- Math Lab works offline after first load, like the Academia app.
- Speed checks on phones.
- **With your approval only:** let Lesson Notes, the Smartboard, Games and Academia activities use the same maths engine, without changing their own tools.
- A final accuracy review of every library entry against known answers, with advanced results flagged for a mathematician to check.

**Significance:** A teacher builds a 3D plane-intersection scene once, sends it to a class, and every student explores it on their own phone, even without data. Math Lab moves from a demonstration tool to a learning tool across all of MathGPL. This is the stage that makes Math Lab part of everyday teaching.
